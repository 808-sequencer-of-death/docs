## Summary & Motivation

This standard is required by Executive Order 14144, as amended by Executive Order 14306.

Cloud service providers nearly always provide guidance to customers with recommended secure configurations for critical services. This standard formalizes requirements and recommendations from FedRAMP about specific recommendations on secure configurations that agency customers should have in advance while setting up a cloud service offering.

This standard will apply to both FedRAMP 20x and FedRAMP Rev5 when formalized, without beta testing for Rev5. FedRAMP anticipates coordinating with all necessary parties to improve this standard over time.

**Background & Authority**  
[Executive Order 14144 “Strengthening and Promoting Innovation in the Nation’s Cybersecurity”](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/17/2025-01470/strengthening-and-promoting-innovation-in-the-nations-cybersecurity) Section 3 (d), as amended by [Executive Order 14306 “Sustaining Select Efforts to Strengthen the Nation’s Cybersecurity and Amending Executive Order 13694 and Executive Order 14144”](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/06/11/2025-10804/sustaining-select-efforts-to-strengthen-the-nations-cybersecurity-and-amending-executive-order-13694) to Section 3 (b), states “*the Administrator of General Services, acting through the Director of the Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program (FedRAMP), in coordination with the Secretary of Commerce, acting through the Director of NIST, and the Secretary of Homeland Security, acting through the Director of CISA, shall develop FedRAMP policies and practices to incentivize or require cloud service providers in the FedRAMP Marketplace to produce baselines with specifications and recommendations for agency configuration of agency cloud-based systems in order to secure Federal data based on agency requirements.*”

## Purpose

FedRAMP’s existing “System Security Plan Appendix J: CSO CIS and CRM Workbook” meets the requirements outlined in this executive order but does not effectively support the underlying intent: all customers benefit from simple, easy to follow, easy to understand instructions for securely configuring a cloud service offering. Cloud service providers often provide a wide range of configuration options to allow individual customers to pick and choose their security posture based on their individual customer needs and are best positioned to provide instructions about the overall security impacts of many of these choices.

This standard outlines simple requirements for FedRAMP authorized cloud service providers to effectively communicate the security impact of common settings to new and current federal agency customers.

# **Definitions**

### FRD-RSC-01

**Top-level administrative account:** The most privileged account with the highest level of access within a *cloud service offering* for a customer organization, typically with complete control over all aspects of the *cloud service offering*, including managing resources, users, access, privileges, and the account itself. 

NOTE: Any references to *top-level administrative accounts* in FedRAMP materials should be presumed to apply to top-level administrative roles or other similar capabilities that are used to assign *top-level administrative account* privileges.

### FRD-RSC-02

**Privileged account:** An account with elevated privileges that enables administrative functions over some aspect of the *cloud service offering* that may affect the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of information beyond those given to normal users; levels of privilege may vary wildly.

NOTE: Any references to *privileged accounts* in FedRAMP materials should be presumed to apply to privileged roles or other similar capabilities that are used to assign privileges to *privileged accounts.*

# **Requirements**

## FRR-RSC

These requirements apply ALWAYS to ALL FedRAMP Authorized cloud services based on the current Effective Date(s) and Overall Applicability of this standard.

### FRR-RSC-01

Providers MUST create and maintain guidance that includes instructions on how to securely access, configure, operate, and decommission *top-level administrative accounts* that control enterprise access to the entire *cloud service offering*.

NOTE: This guidance should explain how *top-level administrative accounts* are named and referred to in the *cloud service offering.*

### FRR-RSC-02

Providers MUST create and maintain guidance that explains all settings that can be operated only by *top-level administrative accounts* and their security implications.

### FRR-RSC-03

Providers SHOULD create and maintain guidance that explains all settings that can be operated only by other *privileged accounts* and their security implications.

### FRR-RSC-04

Providers SHOULD set all settings to their recommended secure defaults for *top-level administrative accounts* and *privileged accounts* when initially provisioned.

### FRR-RSC-05

Providers SHOULD offer the capability to compare all current settings for *top-level administrative accounts* and *privileged accounts* to the recommended secure defaults.

### FRR-RSC-06

Providers SHOULD offer the capability to export all security settings in a machine-readable format.

### FRR-RSC-07

Providers SHOULD offer the capability to view and adjust security settings via an API or similar capability.

### FRR-RSC-08

Providers SHOULD provide recommended secure configuration guidance in a *machine-readable* format that can be used by customers or third-party tools to compare against current settings.

### FRR-RSC-09

Providers SHOULD make recommended secure configuration guidance available publicly.

### FRR-RSC-10

Providers SHOULD provide versioning and a release history for recommended secure default settings for *top-level administrative accounts* and *privileged accounts* as they are adjusted over time.

## vedigaurav — [2025-09-23T20:38:53Z](https://github.com/FedRAMP/community/discussions/84#discussioncomment-14490687)

Thank you for sharing new RFC. Here's some observations and thoughts:

1. Lack of Mandated Format for Machine-Readable Guidance
Gap: FRR-RSC-06 and FRR-RSC-08 require machine-readable output but don’t specify a format (e.g., JSON, YAML, SCAP). This can hinder interoperability and automation.
Recommendation: Define acceptable formats explicitly (e.g., JSON with schema, SCAP, YAML). Include versioning and structure examples. Align with NIST’s OSCAL where feasible.

2. Non-Mandatory Configuration Comparison Capability
Gap: FRR-RSC-05 (“SHOULD offer capability to compare settings”) is optional, yet this is critical for maintaining secure baselines.
Recommendation: Elevate FRR-RSC-05 to a MUST requirement. Mandate inclusion of comparison tools or APIs to support continuous compliance monitoring.

3. No Requirement for Validation or Testing of Provided Guidance
Gap: There's no requirement to validate the correctness, usability, or completeness of the secure configuration guidance.
Recommendation: Add a requirement for CSPs to test and periodically validate secure configuration guidance with sample environments or customer feedback. Encourage FedRAMP to audit a sample of configuration documents for accuracy.

4. No Specific Usability Standard for Configuration Instructions
Gap: While clarity is implied, there’s no standard for ease-of-use or readability in secure configuration guidance.
Recommendation: Require guidance to follow NIST’s usability principles or similar plain-language standards. Include examples or templates to help CSPs align.

5. Lack of Emphasis on Role-Based Access Models Beyond Top-Level Accounts
Gap: Focus is on top-level and privileged accounts, but role-based or scoped permissions are common and unaddressed.
Recommendation: Add guidance on documenting and securing role-based or delegated admin models. Define security expectations for scoped access accounts.

6. Versioning of Configuration Guidance Is Not Mandatory
Gap: FRR-RSC-10 makes versioning optional with "SHOULD," which may reduce traceability and accountability.
Recommendation: Elevate FRR-RSC-10 to a MUST. Require change logs, release dates, and impact summaries for configuration updates.

7. No Alignment with Incident Response or Detection Settings
Gap: The RFC is silent on whether security settings should support audit logging, anomaly detection, or IR readiness.
Recommendation: Include guidance for settings that support auditability (e.g., log retention, alerting). Cross-reference FedRAMP incident response requirements.

8. No Mention of Multitenancy or Shared Responsibility Clarification
Gap: Multitenant CSPs may have platform limitations affecting secure configuration options for customers.
Recommendation: Require providers to document shared responsibility boundaries for each secure setting. Highlight settings not customer-configurable due to platform constraints.

## bthiellen — [2025-10-03T18:27:50Z](https://github.com/FedRAMP/community/discussions/84#discussioncomment-14587039)

Overall I wholeheartedly agree with the proposed standard. I have one minor clarification for FRR-RSC-02.

FRR-RSC-02 → Providers MUST create and maintain guidance that explains all **relevant** settings that can be operated only by top-level administrative accounts and their security implications.

Not every top-level administrative setting carries meaningful security implications. Documenting “all” such settings could lead to guidance that is bloated, less usable, and ultimately less effective for its intended purpose. Instead, focusing on **“all relevant settings”** would ensure that providers deliver clear, actionable guidance that highlights the security impact areas administrators need to understand.

**Rationale for Proposed Change**

* Security Impact Focus: Ensures the guidance emphasizes areas where administrative actions could materially affect confidentiality, integrity, or availability.
* Operational Clarity: Reduces noise by excluding trivial or low-risk settings that provide little security value.
* Usability: More concise and targeted documentation helps administrators quickly identify high-impact areas where mistakes or misconfigurations pose real risks.

This adjustment preserves the intent of the requirement while improving clarity and ensuring security teams focus on what matters most.

## wisecryptic — [2025-10-07T21:19:59Z](https://github.com/FedRAMP/community/discussions/84#discussioncomment-14619722)

Thank you as always for your thoughtfulness in constructing these standards.
For the record, I would like to commend the choices the authors made to keep many of these requirements as a SHOULD instead of a hard requirement, and the reasons I have for making such a commendation.

FRR-RSC-03 - keeps CSPs from feeling obligated to clutter configuration documents with the noise of less important settings that 'might' have a security impact.

FRR-RSC-04 - for certain settings, especially identity provider configurations, it is impossible to set a default initially - agency or customer must input information first, or must configure it themselves with agency specific information that the CSP is not privy to. keeping this requirement as a SHOULD removes the temptation to make highly obtuse,confusing, and unwieldy documentation as a method to protect the CSP from the accusation that they neglected to cover something that does not have a default setting.

FRR-RSC-05 - previously discussed in response to another contributors comments

FRR-RSC-06 - Especially for light SaaS offerings, it would be difficult to use the machine-readable format for certain offerings to any practical purpose. Keeping flexibility focuses the agency and the CSP on the right type of communication instead of the most verbose.

FRR-RSC-07 same as FRR-RSC-06. Additionally, creating an API for settings that are traditionally adjusted manually may inadvertently increase the risk of compromise, hence my commendation on the current wording.

  FRR-RSC-08 - same as FRR-RSC-06. Many CSPs are working on FedRAMP's guidance to automate and streamline security posture. As these developments institutionalize it may be more practical in the future to set a stronger standard.

 FRR-RSC-09 - while generally public availability is best for communication and for trust, there are circumstances where these configuration settings would be better as non-public information. Sometimes the function itself may be public information but recommendations and guidance may expose undue risk to the customer if publicly disclosed, e.g. proprietary endpoints to access privileged functions.

again, thank you for the thoughtfulness of the wording. Keeping these requirements wording as 'SHOULD' will allow agencies and CSPs the flexibility they need while encouraging them to focus on clear communication on the key security concerns.

## sam-aydlette — [2025-11-15T11:57:05Z](https://github.com/FedRAMP/community/discussions/84#discussioncomment-14976441)

Hi FedRAMP team, getting my feedback in. Secure configuration standard should differentiate between two use cases. The first use case is secure configuration guidance provided by the CSP to customers. FedRAMP standards that apply here should define how a vendor provides customers guidance to secure their tenants.

The second use case is secure configuration guidance that a CSP provides to its own internal teams through policies. FedRAMP standards that apply here should define how an organization secures their own configurations. 

The key difference is in who is responsible for applying the configuration. In the first case, the customer applies it, whereas in the second case, the CSP "is" the customer so applies it to their own environment.

For both the first and second use case, I would point to CISA SCuBA as a resource with a "SHOULD" (not a mandate because it's not comprehensive, but where possible it should be used). If not, vendor provided secure configuration guidance is sufficient. In addition to focusing on secure defaults, the RFC should also mention removing unnecessary services, features, and components (i.e., hardening). The line between secure configuration and hardening can sometimes be blurry, so the RFC should just address both in the same place.

Also, I really love the focus on top-level administrative access :) Great stuff. I appreciate you all.
