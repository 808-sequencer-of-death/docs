## **Summary**

This is a technical RFC that proposes updates to the Additional FedRAMP Security Technical Controls Requirements and Guidance for FedRAMP Rev5 baselines.

To save space and to limit the breadth of a single RFC, this technical document shows only the proposed changes to the current baseline document and is restricted to a few control families. If verbiage is being updated, only the specific wording that is to be changed is identified in the old verbiage section. If the verbiage to be added is not replacing previous verbiage, but being added, it will be identified as “NEW” in the old verbiage section.  

Since this spreadsheet covers hundreds of controls across multiple tabs and sections, only those specific controls, sections, and tabs identified are being proposed for updates.  If it is not mentioned below, changes are NOT being proposed.  

**Commenters are advised to please mention specific controls in their comments\!**

This RFC addresses controls in the following families:

* Access Control (AC)  
* Awareness and Training (AT)  
* Audit and Accountability (AU)  
* Assessment, Authorization and Monitoring (CA)  
* Configuration Management (CM)

## **Motivation**

Many Rev5 FedRAMP Requirements and Guidance statements are based on outdated approaches that predated the release of the FedRAMP Authorization Act and M-24-15. This set of Requirements and Guidance needs a refresh to match FedRAMP’s current rules and approach.

The updates formalized after this RFC will be included in the FedRAMP Consolidated Rules for 2026\. That set of rules will be valid until December 31, 2028\. FedRAMP will provide a transition plan for adopting any new guidance that will enable cloud service providers to update their approach as part of their annual assessment as appropriate.

These changes are designed to lower the burden for cloud service providers and eliminate previous pain points.

Additionally, NIST released 800-53 Rev 5.2.0 in August of 2025\.  While this update was minor, control updates from NIST will require updates to FedRAMP Security Control Baseline in order to reflect this update. These changes will happen without public comment through FedRAMP since these are direct reflections of NIST changes. As subsequent iterations of the FedRAMP Security Control Baseline are published, it is FedRAMP’s intent only to carry over the following information from NIST 800-53; Control Family, Control Name, and Control ID. Following this will be FedRAMP-Defined Assignment / Selection Parameters and additional FedRAMP Requirements and Guidance.

## wisecryptic — [2026-03-26T22:28:04Z](https://github.com/FedRAMP/community/discussions/131#discussioncomment-16333900)

CM-06/CM-07

**In short:** I recommend finding a different home for the SCGs because they do not follow the spirit of configuration management under those controls. If they must reside in a control, AC‑2 or AC‑6 are a better fit( My parameter choices would be AC-2d3 or AC-2(7)add'l requirement). This may sound like a small distinction, but getting this right will save a lot of headache in the future for agencies implementing the control, assessors keeping scope of the control, and CSPs tracking the control.

**The longer rationale**
**theoretical discussion**
This proposal represents a significant shift in how these controls are conceptualized. The CM family—Configuration Management—was designed to address baseline configuration control. CM begins with the initial configuration baseline(CM-2), continues with change control (CM-3), and then breaks into more detailed expectations around the state and management of those changes.

CM‑06 is about standardized system configuration settings—establishing what standard configurations are and determining how an organization adheres to them. NIST specifically calls out USGCB, STIGS, etc as examples. 
In contrast, “instructions” that describe the “how to” of accessing, configuring, operating, and decommissioning top‑level accounts are typically part of what most CSPs refer to as implementations. If this material must live in a configuration setting control, we should be looking for recommended configurations, not operational instructions.

CM-07, least function is slightly more nuanced but follows the same basic reasoning. parameters should focus on limiting functionality. "accessing, configuring, operating, and decommissioning" is information far beyond the scope of what CM-07 should be.

**Practical implementation**
CM‑06
CM‑06 Looking at the big picture, I believe that CM-06 could benefit from more flexibility. While STIG implementation should remain an acceptable standalone response, it should also fit into a broader configuration schema that contributes to the configuration baseline (if we are focusing on security practice, not security theatre, that is).
However, the SCG should not live here unless the customer is providing machine‑implementable artifacts or tools that directly configure elements referenced in the SCG. CM‑06 is not the place for instructions or walkthroughs.
CM‑07
CM‑07 Again, thinking big picture, I would change the STIG requirement from “shall” to “may” because:

- STIGs are already required in CM‑06, making the CM‑07 inclusion redundant.
- NIST’s discussion and intent for CM‑07 do not include STIG expectations.
- While STIGs can help satisfy the control, they are not the sole or even primary focus here; the enhancements to CM‑07 clearly point toward a more composite, multi‑layered approach.

Regardless, the SCG should not be inserted in CM-07 either. It distracts from the core intent of least functionality. The SCG is about secure implementation—not least functionality. While the two can overlap, instructions and detailed how‑tos are not examples of least functionality.

So where should the SCG go?
For this particular requirement, I would place it under the AC family, where responsibility for customer‑administered accounts is already delegated. AC‑2 or AC‑6 remain the most logical fits.
However, if FedRAMP anticipates future SCG‑type requirements that fall outside the AC scope but still require explicit assessment, there are two possible approaches:

**Approach 1:** Align each SCG requirement with its corresponding NIST control complement.

- This preserves alignment with NIST’s structure and the community’s shared mental model.
- The downside is fragmentation—SCG content would be distributed across multiple controls.
- A separate guide would be needed to identify where SCG‑related parameters live.



**Approach 2:** Add the SCG requirement to PL‑2 or attach it as a required appendix in the SSP template.

- This keeps related material in one place.
- It also aligns with existing FedRAMP patterns, for better or worse.



This may feel like a small placement issue, but I strongly believe we should not distort the original NIST intent of CM‑06 and CM‑07 with requirements of this nature. While occasionally forcing a “square peg into a round hole” helps expose the need for a new square hole, in this case I think existing compliance scaffolding already provides the right home for this content without bending the CM controls out of shape.

Gap #1: Fragmented Control Update Visibility
Issue: The RFC presents changes across multiple control families in a spreadsheet-style, partial update format, making it difficult to understand cumulative impact across the baseline.
Recommendation: Provide a consolidated “redlined baseline view” or diff summary showing all updates in context to improve traceability and review efficiency.

Gap #2: Insufficient Rationale Depth for Control Changes
Issue: Many updates cite “alignment with Secure Configuration Guidance (SCG)” or BIRs without explaining risk-based justification or expected security outcomes.
Recommendation: Include risk rationale and intended security impact for each control change to help CSPs and assessors interpret intent consistently.

Gap #3: Ambiguity in BIR vs Traditional Path Equivalence
Issue: The RFC introduces alternative requirements for BIR participants (e.g., reduced reporting frequency) without clarifying equivalence in assurance levels.
Recommendation: Define equivalency criteria or assurance benchmarks to demonstrate that BIR-based approaches meet or exceed traditional control objectives.

Gap #4: Lack of Implementation Examples
Issue: Updated requirements (e.g., account types alignment, role-based training enhancements) lack practical implementation examples, increasing interpretation variability.
Recommendation: Provide reference implementations or sample evidence artifacts for selected controls to standardize adoption.

Gap #5: No Explicit Impact on Assessment Procedures
Issue: While control requirements are updated, there is limited guidance on how these changes affect 3PAO assessment methods, test cases, or evidence expectations.
Recommendation: Include assessment guidance updates or mappings to ensure consistent evaluation across assessors.

Gap #6: Incomplete Coverage of Inter-Control Dependencies
Issue: Changes to controls (e.g., AC, CM, CA families) may introduce dependencies or conflicts across control implementations, but these are not addressed.
Recommendation: Document key inter-control relationships and dependency considerations to avoid inconsistent implementations.

Gap #7: Limited Guidance on Automation Alignment
Issue: The RFC aligns with modernization goals but does not explicitly address how updated controls should support automation or machine-readable compliance (e.g., OSCAL).
Recommendation: Include guidance on automation-ready control implementation and reporting, aligning with broader FedRAMP modernization efforts.

Gap #8: Transition Timing and Phasing Ambiguity
Issue: While a transition plan is mentioned, there is no clarity on prioritization, sequencing, or phased adoption of control updates.
Recommendation: Provide a phased adoption roadmap (e.g., control families by priority or impact level) to reduce implementation risk.
