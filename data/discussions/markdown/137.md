> **pete-gov (FedRAMP)** — [2026-04-08T11:34:19Z](https://github.com/FedRAMP/community/discussions/137#discussioncomment-16488230)
>
> It's important to note that templates and documentation for the FedRAMP process are not equivalent in risk to a filled out system security plan for a system, so don't make any assumptions based on how FR makes documentation available!
> 
> That said, the [Minimum Assessment Scope](https://www.fedramp.gov/docs/20x/minimum-assessment-scope/) is designed to allow flexibility in how cloud services leverage third-party independent services based on risk. A key aspect of this is establishing compensating controls with information flows to lower risk.
> 
> In general, a responsibly prepared system security plan and related continuous monitoring materials for a secure cloud service should not be high-risk sensitive materials; in fact, generally one should assume at least a loss of confidentiality when preparing those materials and it's impossible for a cloud service provider to maintain control over them once released to a single party. Integrity can very easily be verified (even automatically) with checksums, and lower availability objectives for security materials can be risk accepted as they don't need to be available 99.999% of the time etc.
> 
> In an extreme example, there is absolutely nothing against the rules with a Class D (High) Certified offering publicly publishing their security materials for the world to see on a non-FR certified service. Done responsibly, especially with a good Vulnerability Disclosure Policy and similar strong vulnerability detection and response policies, they might even be able to prove that doing so increases the security of their service. It's always important to remember that the most of the widely relied-upon critical software that cloud services uses is open-source (linux, kubernetes, etc.). 
> 
> Just like people shouldn't check API keys into their open-source code, people shouldn't put sensitive information that would likely to lead to compromise into security materials. 🤷

## vennemp — [2026-06-18T21:58:25Z](https://github.com/FedRAMP/community/discussions/137#discussioncomment-17357312)

I strongly support FedRAMP's strategic direction with the new Vulnerability Detection and Response framework. Given the current threat environment, including the increasing speed and automation of exploitation activity, a more risk-based and responsive approach to vulnerability management is clearly needed.

That said, I wanted to raise a practical implementation concern that may affect CSPs' ability to meet the required timelines, particularly where agency approval processes do not yet align with the newer Significant Change Notification approach.

The current timeline for CSP adoption, including the December 7 implementation date and the subsequent 90-day grace period, appears to assume that agencies will broadly accept the SCN process for VDR-related system changes. In practice, CSPs may still encounter agency-specific expectations that require use of the more traditional Significant Change Request process (we are seeing this with many agencies on other less significant changes). This creates a potential disconnect between FedRAMP's intended modernization path and how implementation may actually occur across different agency authorization environments.

There are three areas where additional PMO guidance or engagement could materially reduce implementation risk.

### 1. Agency Alignment on SCN Usage

FedRAMP's updated approach to significant changes provides an important opportunity to move from pre-approval-heavy processes toward more continuous, notification-based governance. However, some Agency Authorizing Officials and security teams may continue to require SCRs for changes that CSPs believe are appropriate for SCN handling.

For CSPs working with agencies that take a more conservative approach, the challenge is not unwillingness to comply with VDR expectations. Rather, it is that agency-specific change management expectations may materially extend implementation timelines.

Additional PMO clarification on when VDR-related changes should be handled through SCN, and perhaps even direct engagement with agencies to encourage consistent adoption of that approach, would be very helpful.

### 2. 3PAO Capacity and Cost Considerations

If VDR-related changes are treated as SCRs by many agencies, CSPs may be required to obtain additional 3PAO assessment support. This could create two practical challenges.

First, the cost of additional 3PAO assessment activity can be significant, especially for small and mid-sized SaaS providers that may not have budgeted for a major assessment cycle on this timeline.

Second, the 3PAO ecosystem has finite capacity. If a large number of CSPs are simultaneously directed into SCR-driven assessment activities, there is a real possibility of scheduling backlogs that could delay implementation even for CSPs acting in good faith.

This does not reduce the importance of the VDR requirement, but it does suggest that the success of the rollout may depend on ensuring that the change process is scaled appropriately.

### 3. Avoiding Unintended Marketplace Impact

A rigid enforcement posture, without accounting for agency-level process dependencies or 3PAO capacity constraints, could have unintended consequences for the FedRAMP marketplace.

Some specialized or smaller CSPs may face delays that are outside their direct control. Larger providers may also view repeated high-friction compliance changes as a factor in evaluating the cost and predictability of continued participation in the FedRAMP ecosystem.

The concern is not that enforcement should be weakened. Rather, enforcement should distinguish between CSPs that are not engaging and CSPs that are actively implementing VDR but are delayed by agency process requirements, assessment scheduling, or other documented dependencies.

### Suggested PMO Actions

To support successful adoption while preserving the security intent of the VDR framework, I would respectfully suggest the following:

1. **Provide additional agency-facing guidance on SCN applicability.**
   Clarify that VDR-related implementation changes should generally be handled through SCN where the nature of the change supports that path, unless there is a specific risk-based reason to require an SCR.

2. **Engage directly with Agency AOs and security teams.**
   Help ensure agencies are aligned with FedRAMP's modernization goals and are not unintentionally creating implementation barriers through inconsistent change management expectations.

3. **Establish a conditional safe harbor for good-faith implementation.**
   Consider allowing CSPs that have documented implementation plans, active milestones, and evidence of progress to remain in good standing where delays are caused by agency-directed SCR requirements, 3PAO scheduling constraints, or similar dependencies.

4. **Clarify enforcement expectations.**
   Distinguish between CSPs that are not taking action and CSPs that are actively working toward compliance but require additional time due to factors outside their direct control.

5. **Consider phased enforcement where appropriate.**
   A phased or risk-based enforcement model could help preserve the urgency of VDR adoption while avoiding unnecessary disruption to agencies and the CSP marketplace.

Overall, I fully support the goal of improving vulnerability detection and response across FedRAMP-authorized systems. My concern is that the implementation path needs to account for how agency change management and 3PAO assessment capacity work in practice. With additional PMO guidance and agency alignment, FedRAMP can maintain the urgency of the VDR transition while reducing the risk of avoidable delays, unnecessary costs, or unintended marketplace disruption.
