#Discussion Topic: Reconciling RFC 0008 Flexibility with RFC 0012 KEV-Based Remediation Requirements

## Background

This post is intended to clarify how providers, 3PAOs, and agencies should interpret the remediation expectations outlined in RFC 0008 in relation to the newer requirements introduced in RFC 0012, specifically around the CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog.

## RFC 0008: Mitigation-Based Risk Management

RFC 0008 allows cloud providers to carry vulnerabilities beyond the defined remediation windows when the associated risk has been mitigated. The language explicitly states:

> “Providers MAY carry mitigated vulnerabilities beyond FedRAMP remediation windows for the original risk rating without penalty but MUST remediate such vulnerabilities once it is reasonable to do so.”

This reflects a pragmatic posture: if the operational or security risk has been sufficiently mitigated — for example, through isolation, configuration changes, or compensating controls — then full remediation (e.g., patch deployment) can be deferred, provided it is eventually addressed.

## RFC 0012: Continuous Vulnerability Management and the CISA KEV Catalog

RFC 0012 introduces a formal continuous vulnerability management standard aligned with the CISA KEV catalog. It requires remediation of any KEV-listed vulnerability by the timeline specified in the KEV — typically within 15 to 30 days.

There is no indication in RFC 0012 that mitigation without remediation is acceptable for KEVs. This aligns with broader federal cybersecurity directives that elevate the urgency of KEVs due to their known active exploitation.

## Current Interpretation - please confirm this understanding below:

- **RFC 0008** allows for mitigated non-KEV vulnerabilities to be carried forward when risk is lowered and remediation is tracked appropriately.
- **RFC 0012** mandates strict remediation deadlines for KEV vulnerabilities, regardless of local mitigations.

**Therefore, RFC 0012 takes precedence over RFC 0008 for KEV vulnerabilities.**

## Implementation Suggestion: Tiered Model or similar for clarity

### Tier 1 – KEV Vulnerabilities
- Must be patched by the KEV-defined due date.
- Mitigations do not extend the timeline.
- Exceptions must come from CISA or the PMO.

### Tier 2 – Non-KEV Vulnerabilities
- Follow RFC 0008 guidance.
- Risk-based mitigation can extend remediation timelines.
- Document rationale and mitigation clearly in POA&M and CM deliverables.

##  Supporting Materials Recommendation

Per the FedRAMP site statement that "*Standards will be supported separately by templates, best practices, technical assistance, and other materials*", I suggest the PMO prioritize:

###  Additional Suggested Materials

- **POA&M templates for KEV tracking**, including deadline and remediation fields.
- **Decision tree or flowchart** to determine applicable standard based on CVE source/type.
- **Sample dashboard views** or tagging models to separate KEV and non-KEV vulnerabilities.
- **Integration examples** for CM/POA&M systems and vulnerability management platforms.

**Request for Confirmation**  
Please confirm whether this interpretation — that RFC 0012 requirements supersede RFC 0008 flexibility in the case of KEV vulnerabilities — aligns with PMO expectations, or provide adjustments to clarify any gaps in understanding.

> My personal perspective (speaking for myself) is that there is room for a balanced approach, if we can all agree what "remediation" means for KEVs, and that CSPs have (and, IMO, should be _required_ to have) the capability to determine if there is _even a vulnerability at all_ in the context of their in-use software systems.
> 
> "Remediation without patch" in the suggested matrix above (where it seems the remediation of a KEV is deemed impossible without a software change) lacks the balance that we need.
> 
> The only way to "remediate" a vulnerability is if a vulnerability is present in an in-use software system. All software systems of any meaningful complexity have numerous defects. In one software system, a defect may compromise security objectives, and thus there is a vulnerability present. In another software system, the same identified defect may have no effect on security objectives and there is no vulnerability to remediate. This "_there is no vulnerability present_" condition may be due to an identified input in source code that isn't compiled into a deployed artifact, or modular functionality that is present in storage but not loaded (and not loadable by an attacker), among many other causes.
> 
> One problem with the KEV is how we identify _potential weaknesses_ (defects that may compromise security objectives) in software-intensive systems (using CVEs). We have a goal to map one identified weakness to one identifier via a CVE ID. Entries in the KEV are [required to have a CVE](https://www.cisa.gov/known-exploited-vulnerabilities#:~:text=The%20vulnerability%20has%20an%20assigned%20Common%20Vulnerabilities%20and%20Exposures%20(CVE)%20ID.). KEV entries must also [have clear remedation action](https://www.cisa.gov/known-exploited-vulnerabilities) such as a vendor-provided update.
> 
> When a CVE reads on widely reused software, such as the Linux Kernel, the notion that a KEV entry can crisply articulate the remediation actions goes out the window. Let's look at [CVE-2024-50302](https://tuxcare.com/blog/when-cvss-scores-dont-tell-the-full-story-the-case-of-cve-2024-50302/) as a case study. I'll leave [CVE-2023-44487](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2023/10/10/http2-rapid-reset-vulnerability-cve-2023-44487) (HTTP/2 Rapid Reset) for another day.
> 
> CVE-2024-50302 is part of a vulnerability chain commercialized in Cellebrite mobile device data exfiltration tools. In the context of Android-based devices, it is [known to have been used to target phone of a Serbian student activist.
> ](https://securitylab.amnesty.org/latest/2025/02/cellebrite-zero-day-exploit-used-to-target-phone-of-serbian-student-activist/)
> 
> CISA added CVE-2024-50302 to the KEV on 2025-03-04 with the "Required Action" of "Apply mitigations per vendor instructions, follow applicable BOD 22-01 guidance for cloud services, or discontinue use of the product if mitigations are unavailable." There is an "additional note" on the KEV record, which is not displayed by default on CISA's web site:
> > This vulnerability affects a common open-source component, third-party library, or a protocol used by different products. For more information, please see: https://lore.kernel.org/linux-cve-announce/2024111908-CVE-2024-50302-f677@gregkh/ ; https://source.android.com/docs/security/bulletin/2025-03-01 ; https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln/detail/CVE-2024-50302
> 
> As an information conveyance tool, I find this KEV entry ineffective at advancing the goal of closing this particular known exploited vulnerability. Compare to the New York State Office of Information Technology Services [advisory 2025-025](https://its.ny.gov/2025-025), which is syndicated from the Multi-State Information Sharing and Analysis Center (MS-ISAC) [advisory](https://www.cisecurity.org/advisory/multiple-vulnerabilities-in-google-android-os-could-allow-for-remote-code-execution_2025-025), which is very usable information.
> 
> I sincerely doubt that adding CVE-2024-50302 to the KEV meaningfully improved the security posture of any in-use system, whereas adding "Android OS levels prior to 2025-03-05" to a KEV _may_ have made a meaningful difference. But there's no way for the KEV to do this, because it only identifies vulnerabilities via CVEs, not end-user accessible software identifiers.
> 
> The KEV generally works at the "least common denominator" level of information for both vulnerabilities and remediation guidance ("Apply mitigations per vendor instructions, follow applicable BOD 22-01 guidance for cloud services, or discontinue use of the product if mitigations are unavailable."). That might work when you're dealing primarily with COTS software where there is a single vendor that is responsible for issuing pre-packaged software updates or other end-user-consumable instruction to resolve security concerns about their products (e.g., Sharepoint).

First of all, thank you for providing the opportunity to deliver feedback on FedRAMP’s direction. Overall, FedRAMP is moving in a direction that aligns with how GitHub thinks about risk management, namely the prioritization of `credibly exploitable` vulnerabilities and their actual impact to our organization. This alignment does enable a few positive things to happen:

- We can properly prioritize actual risk to our organization over and above other vulnerabilities that are mitigated or not impactful.  
- Hygiene patching remains important, but we gain a great deal of flexibility with the longer timelines afforded for `All detected vulnerabilities`.  
- Alignment (or enablement) of logical grouping of vulnerabilities. This is already how we operate as an organization, but we have to instead group by asset & CVE for our current con-mon reporting.

The move away from previously difficult to parse language is also greatly appreciated.

That said, we do have some specific feedback that would be useful to drive further clarification within the standard.

## Definitions

### `Credibly Exploitable Vulnerability`

We believe the intent behind `Credibly Exploitable Vulnerability (CEV)` is good. Our concerns stem from a lack of clarity on what constitutes a `CEV`. The way the standard is written, a good deal is left up to the organization and security program to determine. This *is* a good thing, we worry however that this will devolve into requiring large justifications for many vulnerabilities.

A sample criteria or recommendation in an appendix could be a useful tool to guide the conversation between organizations and their sponsoring agencies. While it’s obvious that anything on the CISA KEV list likely belongs as a `CEV`, and likewise if we have another intelligence source giving us reason to believe there is an exploit available then it qualifies. On the less clear side of things, EPSS is a useful tool for triaging and ranking priorities. Is there an EPSS (Exploit Prediction Scoring System) score that should escalate a finding to `CEV`? Probably, but the methodology here is likely to vary highly between organizations.

How does physical access play into this? Do we consider physical exploits as `CEV`? Should we include notes about the security of our datacenters? What about when utilizing cloud vendors?

### Remediation & Mitigation

We think directly referencing the definition in `NIST SP 800-216` makes more sense than re-defining it in this standard.

### False Positives

We often see scanning vendors detect all possible CVEs against a kernel, even when the exploitable kernel module is not present. For example, bluetooth vulnerabilities on Ubuntu kernels. This code is not loaded or present, but due to the way scanning vendors detect CVEs, often they are just included in as a result of basic version matching. Would these be considered `Fully Mitigated` or `False Positives`? We currently consider these `False Positives`.

### Risk Adjustment

Something not covered in the document is the ability to risk adjust vulnerabilities. Currently, we are only allowed to adjust the risk score of a vulnerability by one level (High \-\> Moderate for example). Clarifying that we have greater control to make risk based decisions here would be helpful.
