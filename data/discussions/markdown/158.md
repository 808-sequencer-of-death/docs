# RFC-0032 Offerings By Government

**⁉️NOTE⁉️ 
FedRAMP will not answer questions in this thread as it is reserved for official public comment. If you would like to ask a question or generally discuss this RFC informally, please use the [General Discussion & Q&A on RFC-0032](https://github.com/FedRAMP/community/discussions/157).**

**Status:** Closed  
**Start Date:** August 6, 2026  
**Closed:** September 8, 2026

## **Summary**

Cloud service offerings that are provided by federal government agencies as a shared service for use by other federal agencies are different from cloud service offerings provided by private companies: they are subject to all statutory and policy requirements for federal information systems and must receive an agency authorization to operate following agency-specific requirements. 

The FedRAMP Certification process is designed to establish a standardized process for commercial cloud services operated by private companies to provide agencies with sufficient information to make risk-based decisions when using the cloud service. This process is generally unnecessary for a cloud service that is operated by a federal agency under existing federal requirements.

The Offerings By Government ruleset allows cloud service offerings that are overseen by federal authorizing officials to obtain a FedRAMP Certification following their own agency process without needing to adopt all FedRAMP rules that are designed for private companies. This pathway allows FedRAMP to rely on the authorization, assessment, and ongoing oversight performed by the Agency Provider while preserving the minimum government-wide information and activities necessary to support reuse by customer agencies.

These rules do not constitute a government-wide authorization to operate. Each customer agency remains responsible for determining whether the cloud service offering is appropriate for its use and for authorizing the federal information systems in which the offering will be used.

---

## **Applicability**

These rules apply to a cloud service offering when:

1. The cloud service offering is operated by a federal agency;  
2. The offering is made available for direct use by one or more other federal agencies;  
3. The operating agency maintains responsibility for the security, privacy, assessment, their own authorization, and ongoing monitoring of the offering; and  
4. The responsible Agency Authorizating Official (AO) formally requests use of the Offerings by Government pathway.

For purposes of this ruleset, an Agency Provider is a federal agency responsible for operating a cloud service offering that is made available for use by other federal agencies.

---

## **General FedRAMP Responsibilities**

These rules apply to FedRAMP for granting FedRAMP Certification to cloud services provided by agencies.

### **OBG-FRP-ELG \-Offerings by Government Eligibility**

>FedRAMP MUST apply the Offerings by Government rules only when the responsible Agency Authorizing Official (AO) confirms through official government channels that the cloud service offering meets the applicability requirements of this ruleset.

### **OBG-FRP-EFR Exemption from FedRAMP Rules**

>FedRAMP MUST exempt agency providers from FedRAMP Rules except those in the Offerings By Government ruleset if requested by an agency Authorizing Official during an application for FedRAMP Certification of an agency provided cloud service offering.

### **OBG-FRP-CLS FedRAMP Certification Class Mapping**

>FedRAMP MUST grant FedRAMP Certification at the appropriate agency requested Certification Class UNLESS the request exceeds the FIPS-200 Information System Impact Level in the agency authorization to operate, as follows:
>
>1. Low Impact ATO: Class B  
>2. Moderate Impact ATO: Class C  
>3. High Impact ATO: Class D

### **OBG-FRP-DEC \- Certification Decision**

>FedRAMP MUST review the Offerings by Government Certification Package for completeness and grant FedRAMP Certification when:
>
>1. The Agency Provider meets the eligible requirements;  
>2. The agency Authorization to Operate is current and active;  
>3. The package contains the minimum information required by this ruleset; and  
>4. The package is available to FedRAMP and customer agencies

## **General Agency Provider Responsibilities**

These rules apply to federal agencies providing cloud services to other federal agencies who wish to obtain or maintain FedRAMP Certification.

### **OBG-AGP-ATO Authorization to Operate**

>Agency Providers MUST obtain and maintain an agency authorization to operate for the service offering.

### **OBG-AGP-NAS Notification of Authorization Status**

>Agency Providers MUST notify FedRAMP and customer agencies promptly when the agency authorization to operate is:
>
>1. Allowed to expire;  
>2. Suspended;  
>3. Revoked;  
>4. Replaced by an interim authorization;  
>5. Subject to new material conditions; or  
>6. Otherwise no longer sufficient to support the existing FedRAMP Certification

### **OBG-AGP-CPO Certification Package Overview**

>Agency Providers MUST supply an Agency Certification Package, using any format determined appropriate by the agency,  that includes *at least* the following information:
>
>1. Agency Authorization to Operate letter that includes the FIPS-199 Information System Impact Level  
>2. Agency System Security Plan  
>3. Agency Plans of Action & Milestones  
>4. Agency Privacy Threshold or Impact Assessment  
>5. Agency Security Assessment

### **OBG-AGP-MKT Marketplace Listing**

>Agency Providers MUST supply the necessary information required by [CDS-CSO-PUB](https://www.fedramp.gov/2026/reference/certification-data-sharing/#public-information) to maintain a listing in the FedRAMP Marketplace.

### **OBG-AGP-CCM Collaborative Continuous Monitoring**

>Agency Providers SHOULD establish at least quarterly collaborative continuous monitoring meetings to review ongoing security decisions and risks with their customer agencies.

### **OBG-AGP-UTC Use of Trust Centers**

>Agency Providers MAY use any appropriate trust center or similar sharing mechanism to make the Agency Certification Package available to agency customers and FedRAMP.

## JohnMarshallSSLC — [2026-08-26T16:01:54Z](https://github.com/FedRAMP/community/discussions/158#discussioncomment-18164789)

 
The Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program (FedRAMP) plays a critical role in ensuring that federal agencies adopt secure, reliable, and high performing cloud services. The Shared Services Leadership Coalition (SSLC) appreciates the opportunity to comment on RFC 0032, Offerings by Government, and to provide perspective on how these rules affect the risk management balance between government operated cloud service offerings and commercial cloud service providers.
 
After reviewing the proposed ruleset, we are concerned that the framework establishes two different standards of security—one for federal agencies that provide cloud services to other agencies (“agency providers”) and another for commercial cloud service providers.  This divergence is not merely a matter of administrative burden; it creates real and avoidable security risk. Federal agencies should not be permitted to operate cloud services under a lighter security regime than the commercial providers they compete with. A dual standard approach weakens the overall security posture of the federal enterprise.
 
Moreover, this disparity also creates an uneven playing field between government operated and commercial cloud providers. When government providers face fewer compliance obligations, they gain a significant structural advantage in competing for agency adoption—even when commercial offerings may provide stronger security, better performance, or greater innovation.
 
RFC 0032 exempts agency providers from substantial portions of the FedRAMP rules that commercial providers must fully satisfy. Combined with the ability of agency providers to rely solely on internal authorization processes, this exemption results in a materially lower compliance threshold for government operated cloud services.
 
Commercial cloud providers must undergo the full FedRAMP authorization process, including standardized security assessments, continuous monitoring, documentation obligations, and marketplace transparency rules. These requirements are intentionally rigorous to ensure agencies can make informed, risk-based decisions.
 
By contrast, under RFC 0032:
1.	Agency providers may obtain FedRAMP Certification without adopting the full FedRAMP rules, relying instead on internal agency authorization processes.
2.	Agency providers may submit certification packages in any format they choose, rather than conforming to FedRAMP’s standardized documentation requirements.
3.	Agency providers are exempt from many FedRAMP rules entirely, except for the limited subset included in the Offerings by Government ruleset.
 
This approach lowers the security bar for government operated cloud services at a time when federal cybersecurity threats are increasing in scale and sophistication. It also creates a competitive imbalance: agencies may be incentivized to select government operated services simply because they face fewer compliance obligations, not because they offer superior security or value.
 
Recent Executive Orders reinforce a “commercial first” approach to federal procurement. These directives instruct agencies to:
1.	Prioritize commercially available technologies whenever practicable.
2.	Conduct rigorous market research to identify commercial alternatives.
3.	Justify any decision to procure non commercial or government unique solutions.
4.	Ensure that federal acquisition practices do not disadvantage commercial providers.
 
RFC 0032, as drafted, risks undermining these directives. By allowing agency providers to obtain FedRAMP Certification through a lighter weight process, the ruleset may unintentionally encourage agencies to adopt government operated cloud services even when commercial offerings are more mature, more secure, and more cost effective.
 
To uphold the Administration’s commercial first policy and strengthen federal cybersecurity, FedRAMP should ensure that agency providers meet security and documentation standards equivalent to those imposed on commercial cloud service providers. Equal standards are essential both for security and for fairness.
 
Recommendations
To promote security, fairness, and alignment with federal procurement policy, we respectfully recommend:
1.	Require agency providers to meet the same FedRAMP security assessment and documentation standards as commercial providers, unless a specific statutory exemption applies.
2.	Ensure that FedRAMP Certification for agency providers reflects equivalent rigor, including standardized formats, independent assessments, and continuous monitoring requirements.
3.	Clarify that agencies must still conduct market research and justify any selection of a government operated cloud service, consistent with “commercial first” directives.
4.	Avoid creating incentives for agencies to select government operated cloud services solely because they face fewer compliance obligations rather than because they offer superior performance or value.
 
In conclusion, while RFC 0032 provides a useful framework for recognizing the unique nature of government operated cloud services, the current ruleset risks creating dual security standards that weaken federal cybersecurity and an uneven playing field that disadvantages commercial cloud providers. Both outcomes conflict with the Administration’s commercial first procurement policy.
 
We urge FedRAMP to revise the ruleset to ensure that agency providers are held to standards comparable to those applied to commercial cloud service providers. Doing so will strengthen federal cloud security, promote fairness, and ensure that agencies select the best solutions available—commercial or government operated—based on performance, value, and mission needs.
 
Thank you for considering our comments. We welcome the opportunity to discuss these issues further at your convenience.
 
John Marshall
Founder and CEO
Shared Services Leadership Coalition

## priyanshujha — [2026-08-30T22:37:21Z](https://github.com/FedRAMP/community/discussions/158#discussioncomment-18210657)

_Commenting in my personal capacity. 
Background: I've spent the past decade-plus building security controls for large-scale cloud collaboration and AI systems - customer-managed encryption key services, permission models, and data-residency controls - and I'm a named inventor on U.S. patents in cloud security._

Overall, I support this pathway. Reusing agency-operated offerings under their existing ATOs is pragmatic, and the five-document minimum package keeps the bar low without abandoning transparency. Two suggestions to make reuse decisions easier for customer agencies:

1. OBG-AGP-CPO - add a customer-facing data governance summary to the minimum package

The five required documents describe the provider agency's system and its authorization, but a customer AO evaluating reuse also needs to understand what happens to their agency's data inside the offering. The gap is most pronounced for what is likely the fastest-growing class of government-provided shared services —-AI platforms (e.g., GSA's USAi under OneGov) - whose core function is generating derived artifacts (summaries, transcripts, embeddings, model outputs) from customer agency data. For these offerings, the operative question is not only "does the provider hold a current ATO," but "do my controls follow my data" i.e. Does content derived from a customer document retain the source's access permissions? Can the customer agency control encryption keys for its data? Are audit events for its data available to its own monitoring? Where does derived content live?

Suggested addition as item 6 of the Agency Certification Package (or as expected System Security Plan content):

A summary of customer-facing data governance, including: 

- separation between customer agencies' data
- encryption and key management options available to customer agencies
- whether and how access controls on customer source data are preserved on content the offering derives from that data
- audit log availability to customer agencies; and (e) data location for customer data and derived content.

This is deliberately a disclosure requirement, not a control requirement, it imposes no new engineering obligations on agency providers; it makes existing behavior visible so customer AOs can make informed reuse decisions. A subset could also surface on the Marketplace listing (OBG-AGP-MKT) so agencies can compare offerings before requesting full packages.

2. OBG-AGP-CCM / OBG-AGP-NAS - require notification of material changes to customer data handling

OBG-AGP-NAS covers changes in authorization status, and OBG-AGP-CCM makes quarterly collaborative monitoring a SHOULD. For fast-moving offerings - again, AI services are the clearest case, where models, dependencies, and derived-data flows can change monthly - this leaves a customer agency potentially learning of a material change to how its data is processed only at the next quarterly meeting, or not at all. Suggest a narrow MUST alongside the quarterly SHOULD:

Agency Providers MUST notify customer agencies in advance of changes that materially affect the handling of customer agency data, including changes to encryption or key management, data location, flows of derived content, or third-party model or service dependencies.

This roughly mirrors the significant-change transparency expected of commercial providers - which also partially addresses the "two standards of security" concern raised elsewhere in this discussion: it keeps the two pathways aligned on customer-impacting transparency without importing the full commercial process.

The OBG pathway will succeed to the degree customer agencies can trust reuse without re-assessing from scratch. Both suggestions aim to make that trust inspectable rather than assumed.

Comment from CSOAI LTD (UK 16939677), an independent measurement body that publishes signed, recomputable observations over public AI components.

On the "minimum government-wide information requirements" in RFC-0032: we suggest the minimum set be expressed as machine-readable OSCAL 1.1.0 (`system-security-plan`, `plan-of-action-and-milestones`, `assessment-results`) rather than as document names, so that an agency-operated offering's package can be reused by a customer agency's tooling without re-keying — the same direction RFC-0024 sets for Rev5 providers from September 30, 2026.

Two specifics:

1. `assessment-results` should carry observations with an explicit `determination` field, so that third-party observations — for example over an AI or LLM component the offering depends on — can be imported as evidence without being mistaken for findings. The distinction matters most for components the authorizing official does not control.

2. Each observation should carry a content hash, and where the source is external, a signature. Reuse across agencies is the stated goal of preserving minimum information; a hash and a signature are what let the receiving agency check that what it ingested is what the originating agency published, rather than trusting the transfer.

We make no claim about any offering's control status, and we are not a FedRAMP participant — this is a comment on the machine-readability of the minimum set only.

## ApplyGov — [2026-09-04T18:24:11Z](https://github.com/FedRAMP/community/discussions/158#discussioncomment-18294170)

ApplyGov operates FedHire, a FedRAMP Certified commercial talent acquisition system used by federal agencies. We support FedRAMP's goal of bringing government-operated shared services into the FedRAMP framework and making those cloud services easier for agencies to reuse.

**Our concern with RFC-0032 is straightforward: it would allow offerings by government (OBG) to receive the same FedRAMP Certification as commercial offerings, without requiring the same independent security validation.**

**"FedRAMP Certified" should represent the same level of independent security review and assurance, regardless of whether the service is offered commercially or by the government (OBG).**

Under RFC-0032:

- While commercial offerings must undergo a FedRAMP independent assessment by a FedRAMP Recognized independent assessment service, government-operated offerings would not be required to do so.
- A contractor-built or contractor-operated system would require an independent assessment when offered commercially, but not when offered under an agency's name.
- Customer agencies would be asked to rely on a "FedRAMP Certified" package even though no FedRAMP Recognized independent assessor has validated the offering.

**We recommend that FedRAMP require comparable independent security validation for every offering that receives a FedRAMP Certification.** **If FedRAMP chooses to establish a different assessment model for government-operated offerings, the Certification and Marketplace listing should clearly communicate that difference to customer agencies.**

The key differences between the commercial and proposed government pathways are summarized below.

| **Issue**                                            | **Commercial Offering under CR26**                                                                                                                      | **Government Offering under RFC-0032**                                                               |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Independent security validation<br><br>(OBG-AGP-CPO) | Required through a FedRAMP Recognized independent assessor                                                                                              | Self-prepared; no independent assessor                                                               |
| FedRAMP certification review<br><br>(OBG-FRP-DEC)    | Independent assessment, reviewed by FedRAMP                                                                                                             | Completeness and a current ATO; not assessment quality or independence                               |
| Other FedRAMP requirements<br><br>(OBG-FRP-EFR)      | Apply based on certification class                                                                                                                      | Waived on request                                                                                    |
| Ongoing independent validation<br><br>(OBG-AGP-CCM)  | Persistent independent verification and validation, including FedRAMP independent assessment at least annually for Class B–D; required Quarterly Review | No comparable independent annual assessment required; "should" meet quarterly with customer agencies |
| Marketplace label<br><br>(OBG-AGP-MKT)               | "FedRAMP Certified"                                                                                                                                     | Same "FedRAMP Certified" label                                                                       |

**The difference continues after certification.** Under FedRAMP's 2026 rules, commercial offerings must undergo independent annual assessment and comply with ongoing monitoring requirements. They also must notify FedRAMP and customers of significant changes under FedRAMP's Significant Change Notification process. RFC-0032 contains no comparable requirement of an independent re-assessment by OBGs, makes quarterly collaborative monitoring optional rather than mandatory, and requires no notice to customer agencies when the system materially changes. The result is not simply a different administrative process. It is a different level of independent assurance behind the same FedRAMP Certification.

**Why this matters to customer agencies.** The purpose of independent verification and validation is not simply to add another procedural step. It provides an objective check that the provider's security evidence accurately reflects the implementation and effectiveness of its security controls. FedRAMP's 2026 rules preserve that independent function even as the program moves toward more persistent assessment and monitoring.

RFC-0032 instead permits the entity responsible for operating and authorizing the government offering to supply the security assessment on which FedRAMP Certification will be based. That may be appropriate evidence for the operating agency's own ATO. The question is whether it provides comparable assurance when the same package is given a FedRAMP Certification intended for reuse by other agencies.

**The concern is not hypothetical.** For example, in 2015, a federal agency operating its own system and relying on its self-authorizations and self-managed risk (no 3rd party audits or oversight) suffered intrusions that exposed records on about 22.1 million people, including security-clearance data, after its Inspector General had already flagged systems operating without valid authorization. More recently, in 2025, a federal regulator disclosed that an intruder held access to its email, including senior officials' accounts, for more than a year before an outside alert, not the agency's own monitoring, caught it. In each case the operating agency's own process missed the problem.

**The same system can be judged differently depending on who lists it**. Government offerings may be built, maintained, or substantially operated by commercial contractors. Sold by the contractor, the system would need an independent assessment under CR26; white-labeled under an agency's name, it would not.

That distinction may reflect a difference in legal responsibility, but it does not necessarily reflect a difference in the underlying technical security risk. The "FedRAMP Certified" label would stop meaning the same thing across the Marketplace, mixing independently verified systems with self-attested ones.

**Recommended changes.**

ApplyGov recommends that FedRAMP revise RFC-0032 to:

- **Require independent validation of the Agency Security Assessment.** Require the assessment under OBG-AGP-CPO to be performed or independently validated by a FedRAMP Recognized independent assessment service, using requirements appropriate to the Certification Class.
- **Apply comparable ongoing independent-validation requirements.** Require OBG offerings to maintain independent verification and validation comparable to commercial offerings, including recurring independent assessment appropriate to the Certification Class.
- **Make collaborative continuous monitoring a requirement.** Change OBG-AGP-CCM from **SHOULD** to **MUST**, with requirements appropriate to a shared government service.
- **Apply appropriate Significant Change Notification requirements.** Customer agencies reusing an OBG should receive meaningful notice when material changes affect the security posture or authorization boundary of the service.
- **If FedRAMP does not require comparable independent validation, distinguish the Certification.** The Marketplace should clearly identify when an OBG Certification relies on an Agency Security Assessment that has not undergone FedRAMP independent verification and validation.

We support bringing government-operated shared services into FedRAMP. But the value of FedRAMP reuse depends on agencies knowing what a certification represents. **The same FedRAMP Certified label should not represent different levels of independent security validation depending on who operates—or whose name is placed on—the service.**

## heidie747 — [2026-09-08T19:50:20Z](https://github.com/FedRAMP/community/discussions/158#discussioncomment-18355979)

_We're submitting this as a commercial cloud service provider holding a FedRAMP® Moderate Authorization, currently deployed in production with federal customers, and we want to flag a fixable gap rather than argue against the pathway itself._

There's a legitimate case for a lighter-weight certification route when a cloud service offering is genuinely agency-to-agency, already under continuous federal oversight, and low risk relative to its impact level - RFC-0032's applicability criteria gesture at this but don't operationalize it. As written, though, OBG-AGP-CPO lets an Agency Provider submit a certification package in any format the agency chooses, and the applicability criteria let an internal agency Authorization to Operate substitute for the independent third-party assessment commercial CSOs must complete. That's a real gap in equivalence, not an administrative detail - it changes what the certification is actually verifying.

Our recommendation: keep the Offerings By Government pathway, but hold it to two specific requirements before FedRAMP grants Certification under OBG-FRP-DEC: (1) the assessment must be performed by a party organizationally independent of the operating agency, and (2) the control baseline must map directly to the same FedRAMP® baseline required of commercial CSOs at that impact level, even if the package format itself remains agency-determined. That preserves the speed and flexibility this pathway is meant to offer agencies, while keeping "FedRAMP Certified" a consistent signal regardless of who operates the service.

We partner with government and commercial industry in the fight to reduce the vulnerabilities in the cyber infrastructure of our nation.

These comments come directly from four years as the Independent Assessment Service for the Treasury TCloud Moderate and TCloud High cloud service offerings, a large multi-bureau shared cloud service of exactly the type this ruleset is designed to accommodate. They also draw on lessons learned we shared with Treasury last year regarding FedRAMP assessment support for the EBSD WC2 shared services platform, which serves multiple Treasury operating divisions as relying parties. That engagement surfaced three operating dynamics that bear directly on this ruleset: continuous versus episodic assessment, significant change management, and multi-tenant onboarding.

Excentium supports the policy objective of RFC-0032. Asking agency-operated shared services to fully replicate a commercial FedRAMP assessment before other agencies may reuse them creates a real barrier to efficient government-to-government cloud sharing, and a lighter-weight pathway that leans on existing agency ATO authority is a sound design choice. The recommendations below are offered to strengthen the assurance a customer agency receives when it relies on an Offerings By Government (OBG) certification, while preserving the streamlined intent of the rule.

A note on terminology: these comments use "Independent Assessment Service" throughout, following the shift FedRAMP made in `FRD-ASR` away from the historical term "Third Party Assessment Organization."
### R1. Independence of the Agency Security Assessment

**Ref:** *`OBG-AGP-CPO` (Certification Package Overview), item 5*

`OBG-AGP-CPO` requires an Agency Security Assessment inside the Agency Certification Package, and leaves open who performs it and what separation exists between that function and the teams that operate and develop the offering.

Under the standard pathway, independence carries structural weight that a customer AO can lean on. `REC-IAS-ACC` requires an assessor to obtain and maintain accreditation through the A2LA Cybersecurity Inspection Body Program. `REC-IAS-SEP` bars an assessor from assessing an offering within two years of supplying advisory or consulting services for that offering. Recognition is then sustained through annual A2LA surveillance and a full reassessment at least every two years. That apparatus is the reason one agency can place weight on an assessment performed for someone else. For a shared service the size of TCloud, serving numerous customer bureaus and agencies, the identity and independence of the assessor directly shapes how much weight a customer AO can reasonably place on the certification.

> **Recommendation:** Require the Agency Security Assessment to be performed by a function organizationally independent of the system's operations and development teams, consistent with the assessor independence principles in NIST SP 800-53A, and require the Agency Certification Package to disclose who performed it. Naming the function is sufficient: an internal agency assessment team, an Inspector General function, or a voluntarily engaged FedRAMP Recognized Independent Assessment Service.
### R2. Certification Class Mapping: FIPS Reference Alignment and Risk Adjustment

**Ref:** *`OBG-FRP-CLS` (FedRAMP Certification Class Mapping); `OBG-AGP-CPO` item 1; `FRD-SCT`*

There are two points here, one clerical and one substantive.

**First, the FIPS references diverge inside the draft.** `OBG-FRP-CLS` conditions the Certification Class on the "FIPS-200 Information System Impact Level" in the agency ATO, while `OBG-AGP-CPO` item 1 requires an ATO letter that includes the "FIPS-199 Information System Impact Level." CR26 already carries a defined term for this concept: `FRD-SCT` (Security Category) takes its meaning from NIST FIPS 199 and notes that security categories are commonly called impact levels. Aligning both OBG rules on `FRD-SCT` resolves the ambiguity before it reaches an AO.

**Second, the class mapping operates as a ceiling.** `OBG-FRP-CLS` grants the agency-requested class unless the request exceeds the impact level in the ATO. Ceiling logic works cleanly for a single-tenant offering. It carries less information for shared infrastructure. A Moderate offering used by one bureau and a Moderate offering functioning as shared infrastructure for a dozen customer agencies, as TCloud does, present very different aggregate risk profiles under an identical security category. Timing raises the stakes on getting this right at the outset: `SCN-CSO-EVA` establishes that a Certification Class change requires a new assessment and cannot proceed under the Significant Change Notification rules, so the class assigned at certification tends to hold for the life of the offering.

> **Recommendation:** Give FedRAMP an explicit risk-adjustment mechanism at the point of certification. Where scope, tenancy, or the breadth of downstream reuse warrants heightened scrutiny, FedRAMP should be able to document that determination and either ask the responsible AO to revisit the security category, or attach specific conditions to the certification, such as expanded package content, a defined collaborative continuous monitoring cadence, or a defined reassessment interval.
### R3. Marketplace Listings Should Show the Pathway and the Assessing Function

**Ref:** *`OBG-AGP-MKT` (Marketplace Listing); `CDS-CSO-PUB`; `MKT-CSO-MLR`*

A customer agency making a reuse decision benefits from knowing, at the moment of decision, whether a Marketplace listing reflects a certification assessed by a FedRAMP Recognized Independent Assessment Service or an OBG certification grounded in an agency's own ATO.

The mechanism for surfacing this already exists in the data model. `OBG-AGP-MKT` routes Agency Providers through `CDS-CSO-PUB`, whose required public information includes the current FedRAMP Recognized Independent Assessment Service. For an OBG certification, that field has no natural occupant under the draft as written, and the ruleset is silent on how to populate it.

> **Recommendation:** Require Marketplace listings for OBG certifications to carry a visible pathway indicator, and require the existing `CDS-CSO-PUB` assessor field to name the function that performed the Agency Security Assessment. Where an Agency Provider engaged a FedRAMP Recognized Independent Assessment Service, that field carries the information already, with no change to the schema.
### R4. Collaborative Continuous Monitoring Should Be a MUST

**Ref:** *`OBG-AGP-CCM` (Collaborative Continuous Monitoring); `CCM-QTR-MTG`; `CCM-OCR-AVL`*

`OBG-AGP-CCM` sets quarterly collaborative continuous monitoring meetings as a SHOULD. Under the standard pathway, `CCM-QTR-MTG` makes the Quarterly Review a MUST for Class C and Class D certifications. `OBG-FRP-CLS` maps a Moderate ATO to Class C and a High ATO to Class D, so an OBG offering will commonly sit at a class where the standard pathway treats the meeting as mandatory.

Ongoing collaboration also does more work under this pathway than it does under the standard one, because the OBG package rests on the agency's own assessment activity. The meeting is where a customer agency sees the current state of the service it depends on.

> **Recommendation:** Elevate `OBG-AGP-CCM` to a MUST for offerings certified at Class C and Class D, and specify minimum content for the review: open POA&M status, a vulnerability scan summary, significant change notices since the last review, and an incident summary. `CCM-OCR-AVL` offers a ready model for that content, including its requirement for a list of all agencies directly using the product, which is information a customer agency needs in order to understand the shared environment it has joined.
### R5. Define "Promptly"

**Ref:** *`OBG-AGP-NAS` (Notification of Authorization Status); `SCN-TRF-NAF`; `SCN-TRF-NAV`*

`OBG-AGP-NAS` requires notification "promptly" when the agency ATO expires, is suspended, revoked, replaced by an interim authorization, made subject to new material conditions, or otherwise becomes insufficient to support the certification. An undefined window will be read differently across agencies. For suspension or revocation, a customer agency needs a firm, short period in which to make its own reliance and continued-use decisions.

CR26 already supplies windows to align with. `SCN-TRF-NAF` and `SCN-TRF-NAV` both set 5 business days for notification after finishing and after verifying a transformative change. Incident communication under the IEC rules runs tighter still, at 1 business day for the most consequential PAIN levels.

> **Recommendation:** Define "promptly" as within 5 business days of the triggering event, and consider a 1 business day window for suspension and revocation, where a customer agency's continued use of the service is most directly at stake.
### R6. Specify a Reassessment Cadence

**Ref:** *`OBG-FRP-DEC` (Certification Decision); `OBG-AGP-ATO`; `IVV-CSO-FIA`*

`OBG-FRP-DEC` conditions certification on an agency Authorization to Operate that is current and active. The ruleset sets no cadence for refreshing the underlying Agency Security Assessment. An ATO can remain administratively current for years while the control environment moves underneath it.

Under the standard pathway, `IVV-CSO-FIA` requires providers at Class B, Class C, and Class D to complete an independent verification and validation assessment at least once per year. That annual rhythm is what keeps a certification meaningful in the long stretches between authorization milestones.

This concern is grounded in delivery experience. Across four years supporting FedRAMP assessment work for a comparable Treasury shared services platform, we consistently found that a cadence tied to the administrative currency of an authorization understates the effort required to keep a shared service's authorization basis current. It also sits at some distance from FedRAMP's own direction of travel, which has moved steadily toward continuous authorization and machine-readable, continuously current security data.

> **Recommendation:** Require the Agency Security Assessment to be refreshed at least annually and upon significant change, and require the Agency Certification Package to state the assessment date so a customer agency can judge currency at the moment of reliance.
### R7. Inheritance by Leveraging Commercial Offerings

**Ref:** *General, with no corresponding OBG rule; `MAS-CSO-TPR`; `OBG-AGP-UTC`*

Agency-operated shared services are frequently inherited as underlying infrastructure by other FedRAMP Certified offerings. A commercial provider building a Moderate or High offering atop agency-provided infrastructure is a common pattern and a likely growth area under this pathway.

Under the standard pathway, `MAS-CSO-TPR` requires that provider to document, for each third-party information resource: general usage and configuration, explanation or justification for use, mitigation measures in place, and compensating controls in place to reduce potential impact to federal customer data. Satisfying that rule requires evidence from the underlying provider. RFC-0032 places no corresponding obligation on the Agency Provider, and `OBG-AGP-UTC` makes trust center sharing a MAY.

> **Recommendation:** Add a provision requiring Agency Providers to make relevant Agency Certification Package evidence available to the Independent Assessment Service assessing a leveraging offering, at a level of detail sufficient to satisfy `MAS-CSO-TPR` and to support the customer responsibility documentation the leveraging provider owes its own agency customers.
### R8. Scope of "Other Federal Agencies" for Intra-Department Shared Services

**Ref:** *Applicability items 2 and 4; `OBG-FRP-ELG`; `FRD-AGY`*

Many of the largest agency-operated shared services, TCloud among them, are offered by one bureau or component for use by other bureaus or components inside the same department.

The applicability language turns on making the offering available "for direct use by one or more other federal agencies." CR26 defines Agency in `FRD-AGY` by reference to 44 U.S.C. § 3502(1), which reaches executive departments, military departments, government corporations, and independent regulatory agencies. Read against that definition, the applicability language points toward cross-department sharing, which leaves the intra-department case open to interpretation at exactly the scale where this pathway would deliver the most value.

> **Recommendation:** State in the final rule whether bureau-to-bureau or component-to-component shared services within a single department fall within the definition of Offerings By Government. If they do, clarify whether the responsible AO under `OBG-FRP-ELG` may sit at the bureau level or must sit at the department level.
### R9. Voluntary Engagement of an Independent Assessment Service

**Ref:** *`OBG-AGP-CPO` item 5; `OBG-AGP-MKT`; `REC-IAS-ACC`*

Nothing in the draft prevents an Agency Provider from voluntarily engaging a FedRAMP Recognized Independent Assessment Service to perform the Agency Security Assessment, and nothing invites or credits it either. For offerings with wide downstream reuse, an agency may want the option to strengthen assurance while staying on this streamlined pathway.

Excentium performs this work commercially, and we note that interest openly. We offer the recommendation because the assurance value accrues to customer agencies regardless of which recognized firm an Agency Provider chooses.

> **Recommendation:** State explicitly that an Agency Provider may voluntarily engage a FedRAMP Recognized Independent Assessment Service to perform the Agency Security Assessment, and that doing so may be reflected as an independence indicator on the Marketplace listing through the existing `CDS-CSO-PUB` field, with the certification remaining grounded in the agency ATO.
### R10. Significant Change Assessment for Agency-Operated Offerings

**Ref:** *General, with no corresponding OBG rule; cf. `OBG-AGP-NAS`; `SCN-CSO-EVA`*

The standard pathway asks providers to evaluate and classify changes before implementing them. `SCN-CSO-EVA` sets that evaluation, sorting each change into a Certification Class change, a routine recurring change, a transformative change, or an adaptive change, with distinct notification windows attached to each. `OBG-AGP-NAS` reaches only changes in the status of the underlying ATO. A significant change to the offering's architecture, boundary, or control implementation, made while that ATO remains active, falls outside its scope.

Our experience supporting continuous authorization and significant change assessment for a large Treasury shared services platform is that this is precisely where ongoing risk accumulates. Changes arrive continuously between formal authorization milestones. A rule scoped to ATO status will pass over them.

> **Recommendation:** Require Agency Providers to maintain a documented significant change process comparable to the SCN rules, and to notify FedRAMP and customer agencies when a significant change materially affects the offering's authorization boundary or risk posture, alongside the ATO status notifications `OBG-AGP-NAS` already requires.
### R11. Onboarding Additional Customer Agencies Should Trigger a Boundary Review

**Ref:** *Applicability item 4; `OBG-FRP-ELG`; `CCM-OCR-AVL`*

Shared-service offerings expand the population of customer agencies they serve over time. Supporting a comparable multi-tenant Treasury shared services platform, we watched each newly onboarded customer introduce integration points and data flows, and in several cases new capabilities added specifically to accommodate that customer. Any of those can move the offering's risk profile while the Agency Provider's own ATO stays exactly where it was.

RFC-0032 conditions eligibility on the AO's confirmation under `OBG-FRP-ELG` at the outset and stays silent on what happens as the tenant population grows. The standard pathway already treats the tenant population as material information: `CCM-OCR-AVL` requires providers to report a list of all agencies directly using the product every three months.

> **Recommendation:** Require Agency Providers to assess whether onboarding a new customer agency constitutes a significant change to the OBG-certified offering, and to document that determination in the ongoing Agency Certification Package. Treating the `OBG-FRP-ELG` confirmation as a living determination keeps the eligibility basis current as the offering grows.
### R12. Certification Type and Path for OBG Certifications

**Ref:** *General, with no corresponding OBG rule; `OBG-FRP-EFR`; `OBG-FRP-CLS`; `FRD-CPH`*

RFC-0032 establishes what an OBG certification rests on and leaves open where it sits inside the CR26 structure. Two attributes go unstated: the Certification Type, meaning 20x or Rev5, and the Certification Path.

Each attribute carries consequences. `FRD-CPH` describes the Agency Certification path as a legacy path available only for the Rev5 type, still subject to FedRAMP review and approval. Because an OBG certification rests on an agency Authorization to Operate, it structurally resembles that path. At the same time, `OBG-FRP-EFR` exempts Agency Providers from FedRAMP rules outside this ruleset, which sets aside the Rev5 control obligations that ordinarily define the Rev5 type. As the rules read today, an OBG certification fits neither type cleanly.

Timing sharpens the question. `OBG-FRP-CLS` maps a High impact ATO to Class D. Under CR26 today, Class D is reachable through Rev5, with 20x Class D noted as arriving in 2027, and FedRAMP stops accepting applications for new Rev5 Certifications on June 11, 2027. A High-impact agency shared service such as TCloud High is exactly the profile this pathway is built to serve, and it is also the profile most exposed to that date.

We offer this as a clarification request. The final rule may well contemplate an answer that the draft simply does not state.

> **Recommendation:** State in the final rule which Certification Type and which Certification Path an OBG certification occupies, and confirm that the pathway remains available for new Agency Provider certifications after June 11, 2027. Where Offerings By Government functions as a distinct path alongside Program Certification and Agency Certification, naming it as such in `FRD-CPH` would let agencies filter Marketplace listings accurately and would keep the OBG data model consistent with the rest of CR26.
### Closing

Excentium appreciates the opportunity to comment on RFC-0032 and welcomes the chance to discuss any of these recommendations further with the FedRAMP PMO.

Shared services that government builds once and reuses across the enterprise are among the most efficient security investments available to federal agencies. Getting the assurance model right at the outset is what allows that reuse to scale with confidence. That is work worth doing carefully, and it traces back to the reason we do any of this: to reduce the vulnerabilities of our Nation.
